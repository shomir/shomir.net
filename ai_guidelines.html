Generative AI Guidelines for Students in HLT Lab

This page is part of my advice pages.

Research is the creation of human knowledge, and its defining qualities include human knowing and our ability to share the experience of knowing. These qualities require a variety of human activities, and AI does not replace the value you bring to them. My research advisees should follow the guidelines below about generative AI usage or ask me before deviating from them:

  • Manuscripts: The activity of writing has value beyond communication, because it sharpens and sometimes changes our understanding of a topic. First drafts should be yours. Please do not ask AI to write for you, and do not ask it to rewrite for you beyond the scope of an individual sentence (which itself should be a rare request). After you write, you may seek AI feedback to improve your grammar, spelling, style, and word choice. Note that some publication venues may have rules about AI writing in the call for papers or in related instructions. See the section "Generative AI and Writing" in my Writing Guide for more perspective.
  • Emails: If an email feels long or you're having difficulty expressing yourself, the solution is probably rethinking the message or having a conversation, not AI (or an email). Using AI to copyedit is fine—I appreciate well-written emails—but the first draft should be yours.
  • Literature Reviews: AI can accelerate the discovery of related work, but it can also ignore relevant papers, misunderstand claims, or fabricate sources. If you use AI for a literature search then also use a scholarly search engine (i.e., searching with keywords you decide are relevant), allowing the two methods to complement each other. When AI suggests a source for a claim, verify yourself that the source exists and actually makes that claim. Use the publisher's BibTeX for a paper if at all possible. AI sometimes omits authors or corrupts paper titles when reciting references (in BibTeX format or otherwise), which can cause paper submissions to be desk rejected and authors to be banned.
  • Presentations, inside and outside the lab: Making slides about research helps you to choose the most important things to say and to reflect on the best way to communicate them. Remember that your speaking and your interactivity should drive a presentation, and slides should be tools to help an audience understand you. Please do not ask AI to create slides for you, and avoid using AI-generated text, figures, or images (unless they are examples to illustrate AI output). You may seek feedback from AI on the contents of your slides. Please do not ask AI to prepare a script for you to speak or to speak in place of you. The same guidelines apply to poster presentations.
  • Ideating and Research Strategy: How we relate to ideas depends in part on whether they came from us or an external source. The creative ideas that guide your work should be primarily yours, and (human) collaborators should be your main sources of feedback. If you must seek AI feedback, reflect on the extent to which the ideas remain yours. Also be aware of the data practices of the AI services you use, and avoid divulging confidential or sensitive information if it may become part of a model’s training data.
  • Programming: AI can be a tool for working with programming languages, but AI should not decrease or substitute for your understanding of algorithms, data, analysis methods, or research problems. Avoid using AI for tasks if you cannot easily verify the results. Remember you bear the responsibility for source code that you generate using AI, and complacency can have devastating (even if delayed) consequences. Search for and read best practices for coding with the specific AI tool that you use.
  • Mentorship: Helping my research advisees with their professional development is one of my top priorities. It's also one of my favorite work activities, and I am willing to make time for it even if I appear to be very busy. Unsurprising career advice from AI has little value, and surprising advice deserves confirmation from a knowledgeable person. Additionally, the act of seeking advice from a mentor is itself a learning experience, as you must reflect on your relationship with them to situate your questions and interpret the answers. Interacting with a human mentor supports your interpersonal skills, which are important for personal and professional growth.

When working on lab projects with collaborators who are external to my lab, discuss acceptable AI use early to avoid complications. You can share these guidelines to help structure the conversation. If an external collaborator disagrees with these guidelines, check with me for further instructions. Avoiding irresponsible AI use is a shared responsibility, and I would rather have a project or manuscript paused while you check with me than discover problems later.

Finally, you may encounter vague claims that create social pressure to use AI (e.g., that everyone is doing it, or if you don't then you will fall behind). Carefully evaluate the motivations behind those claims, the evidence for them, and how they relate to your values. Similar claims about topics other than AI have misled people before. When in doubt, it's better to err toward exercising human judgment than to err toward overautomation.
